Data types · Concept

Concept Two questions sort any dataset: what is one record, and how are the records related to each other.

three ways records relate
What is one record. A row with fixed columns, a set of items, a number with a time stamp, a sentence, a node with its links.
Why it matters. Most methods in modules 5 to 7 expect a table of independent rows. Data in any other form has to be converted to one, or needs a method built for its form.

Data types · Example: sequence

Example continued 3 The 7 baskets of customer C01921, placed in the order they were bought, form a sequence.

customer C01921 · January to June 2026
A sequence is ordered events, with or without exact times. 1,995 customers have at least one basket; the median is 6 baskets and the largest is 16.
A question only a sequence can answer. Do customers who buy nappies start buying something else a few weeks later? Sequential pattern mining, module 8.

Example continued 4 Make each item a node and join two items when they share baskets. The 14 items give 91 possible links.

Two links stand out. Butter and jam (lift 3.38) and beer and nappies (lift 2.76). 87 of the other 89 pairs have a lift between 0.9 and 1.1.
A record here is a node and its links. Social networks, payment networks and the web have the same form with millions of nodes.

Data types · Example: stream

Example continued 6 Seen from the tills, the sales file was never a file. It arrived one row at a time and it has no last row.

the last six rows: all that the method keeps
One pass. A stream is read once, in arrival order. There is no going back to sort it or to compute a second statistic.
Bounded memory. The method keeps a summary of fixed size: a running count, a running total, a window of the latest rows.
An answer at any moment. A fraud check on a card payment has to return before the payment completes.
Not in these files. Spatial data (store locations, delivery routes) and multimedia (shelf cameras, call recordings) are two more forms. Module 8 takes streams and both of these.

Data types · Drawbacks

Drawbacks Each of the five non-tabular types can be turned into a table, and each conversion throws away the property that made the type what it is.

converting to a table
Conversion is the first thing to try. It lets every method of modules 5 to 7 run on the data.
When the lost property is the question, a method built for the type is needed: sequential patterns for order, graph methods for paths, time-series models for what came before the window.
Mining tasks · Concept

Concept A mining task is defined by the kind of pattern it returns. The textbook lists seven, in two groups.

Han, Kamber & Pei, section 1.4
The next seven slides run each task once on the supermarket files and show its input, its output and one number that says how well it did.
Supervised or not. Classification and numeric prediction need a column of known answers to learn from. The four descriptive tasks need none.

Mining tasks · Classification

Example continued 2 Classification learns to predict a category from the other columns. A two-level decision tree predicts whether a basket contains jam and is right on 85.4% of test baskets.

the tree's first split · and its results on baskets it had not seen
Input. A table with one column marked as the answer: jam, yes or no. 70% of baskets train the tree and 30% test it.
Accuracy needs a baseline. Predicting "no jam" for every basket is right 82.1% of the time. The tree adds 3.3 points.
Where it fails. It finds 325 of the 646 jam baskets and misses 321. Module 6 covers trees, naive Bayes and how to score them.
Mining tasks · Numeric prediction

Example continued 3 Numeric prediction returns a number. A straight line predicts the amount of a basket from how many different items it holds: each extra item adds ₹174.

R squared of 0.33. The line explains a third of the variation between baskets. The rest is which items: a basket with nappies costs ₹699 more than one with chips.
Input. A table with a numeric answer column. Regression is the standard method; module 6.
Mining tasks · Clustering

Example continued 4 Clustering forms groups that were not defined beforehand. Here the 14 items are grouped by how often they share a basket; butter and jam merge first.

The measure decides the groups. Bread and milk merge second because each is in most baskets, although their lift is below 1. Beer and nappies, lift 2.76, merge eleventh.

Mining tasks · Outlier detection

Example continued 5 Outlier detection returns the records that do not fit the rest. Tukey's rule on basket amounts flags 635 of the 12,000 baskets, those above ₹1,355.

baskets by amount, in steps of 200 rupees
median₹360
flagged635
largest₹3,493
What the outliers are. 482 of the 635 contain nappies, which cost ₹699 a pack. They are ordinary baskets with one expensive item.
Input. Records and a notion of what is usual. Whether an outlier is an error, a fraud or a good customer is decided afterwards. Module 7.
Mining tasks · Trend analysis

Example continued 6 Trend and evolution analysis describes how values change with time. On Fridays the share of baskets with nappies changes at 5 pm.

Fridays · share of baskets containing nappies, by hour
Output. A change point: before 5 pm the share is 1% to 8%, from 5 pm it is 25% or more.
The count hides it. Fridays have 66.7 baskets a day against 67.0 on Thursdays. The number of baskets does not move; what is in them does.
Input. Records with a time stamp, kept in order. Module 8.
